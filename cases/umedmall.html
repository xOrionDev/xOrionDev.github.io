Project Overview

One marketplace, three specs, written months apart by different people.

UmedMall is a multi-category marketplace built for the Tajik market, benchmarked by the client against OZON, Wildberries, Yandex.Market and Etsy. By the time I joined, a client-side spec already existed. The moderator spec arrived next. The seller/partner spec didn't exist at all — "we'll send it later."

My job was to make sure none of the three surfaces contradicted each other: one taxonomy, one attribute model, one source of truth — expressed through three different interfaces, for three different people, with three different jobs to do.

I designed all three: the client storefront, the moderator console, and the seller cabinet — research, information architecture, content modeling, UI, and Figma hand-off.

The Problem

The brief described a database. It needed to describe a workflow.

Business reality

The client's three specs were written sequentially, by different people, with no shared vocabulary. The client-facing catalog and the moderator's "Characteristics" page used different words for the same idea. The seller cabinet wasn't specified at all when I started designing the moderator side.

Three things were true at once:

  • The moderator spec was, almost literally, a list of database tables: Orders, Categories, Characteristics, Banners, Administrators, Stores.
  • No one had designed how a seller actually lists a product without breaking the catalog for everyone else.
  • "Improve it however you think best" was the actual brief — a blank check that's easy to under-deliver on.

The literal brief, verbatim

This is the entire moderator navigation as originally specified — six flat categories, no grouping, no hierarchy, no mention of units, input types, audit logs, cities, or brands. Sellers were expected to type product attributes as free text.

The original sidebar, as briefed:

"Заказы. Категории. Характеристики. Баннеры. Администраторы. Магазины."

— Orders. Categories. Characteristics. Banners. Administrators. Stores. The whole moderator product, in six words.

Grounding the rebuild

No interviews, no usability lab — so I borrowed conviction from competitors and stress-tested the data model instead.

There was no budget or access for primary user research. So I grounded every decision in two things I could control: how mature marketplaces actually structure this problem, and how badly a naive schema would break under real-world data.

Method 01

Competitive IA audit

Walked the moderator/admin patterns of OZON, Wildberries, Yandex.Market and Etsy — not for visuals, but for how they group catalog management, order processing, and seller onboarding. Reused validated structure instead of inventing one from a flat spec.

Method 02

Cross-spec reconciliation

Read the client-side spec, the moderator spec, and (once it arrived) the seller spec against each other, line by line, looking for contradictions before they became dev tickets — e.g. a "verified store" badge the client side referenced that the moderator side never defined.

Method 03

Edge-case stress-testing

Before designing the attributes screen, I deliberately pushed it with hard cases: temperature in °C/°F/K, length in cm vs. m vs. inches, a washing machine's "8kg, white, automatic" typed as one free-text string. Each broke something — which told me what the schema actually needed to prevent.

Key insights

Insight 01

A literal spec is a schema, not an IA

Six flat top-level items copied from table names hide nineteen real sections. No single moderator should face that list ungrouped.

Insight 02

Free text kills filters before they're built

"Automatic, 8kg, white" typed as one string by one seller can never be filtered, compared, or sorted against the same attributes typed differently by another seller.

Insight 03

Approval needs state integrity, not just a status field

"Approved" and "store exists" must be the same fact, not two fields that can drift apart. The spec never said this — the data model demanded it.

"What if I stopped operating strictly from the spec, and designed what the product actually needed?" — The question I kept asking myself, three weeks in
Define

The brief described three interfaces. The real problem was one trustworthy attribute engine underneath all of them.

Problem statement

The client storefront, the moderator console, and the seller cabinet each needed to trust the same taxonomy and the same product attributes — without giving sellers enough freedom to enter data that breaks filtering, comparison, or search downstream.

Solve that once, at the data-model level, and three very different interfaces become straightforward to design on top of it.

Goals

  • Unify categories, characteristics and units under one moderator-owned schema that client and seller surfaces simply consume.
  • Replace the literal flat sidebar with navigation grouped by how moderators actually triage work, not by table name.
  • Close every approval loop with a mandatory, visible reason — not just a status change nobody can audit.
  • Design the seller cabinet from near-zero spec, mirroring the moderator's data model so every listing imports cleanly.

What "done" had to mean

No flat lists

19 moderator sections collapse into 7 task-based groups, none with more than 4 top-level items.

Zero free-text attributes

Every product attribute is an enum, a number with a unit, or a boolean — never an open string a seller can type anything into.

Every rejection has a reason

Store and product rejections require a mandatory reason, visible to other moderators — no more "why was this declined?" pings.

Process

The decisions nobody asked for are the ones I'm proudest of.

None of the four decisions below were in the original spec. Each one came from asking "what happens when this breaks at scale" before it actually did.

1

A two-level attribute system: moderators define the schema, sellers fill constrained values

Sellers don't get a free-text field for "color" or "capacity." A moderator first defines the characteristic (its input type, its unit, its validation rules) once, globally. Sellers then pick from that fixed vocabulary when listing a product. This single decision is what makes filtering and comparison possible across thousands of listings from hundreds of independent sellers.

2

Base units + conversion, even for attributes nobody asked to compare

Every unit category (length, mass, volume, power, temperature) stores a single base unit internally — Kelvin for temperature, millimeters for length — and converts on display. A seller can enter 25°C or 77°F; the system stores 298.15K either way. Without this, "filter fridges under 90cm wide" silently fails for every seller who typed "0.9m."

3

Collapsing 19 flat items into 7 named, iconified groups — and showing my work

I presented three real alternatives — 6 groups (maximally compact), 7 groups (balanced), and 9 groups (maximally explicit) — with the actual trade-off named out loud: fewer groups for a small moderation team wearing many hats, more groups if responsibilities split by role. The client picked 7, and recognized every group name because it matched their own vocabulary back.

4

Caught a state-integrity bug before it shipped: "approved" must equal "store exists"

The original spec allowed a store application to be marked "Approved" as a status change, separately from actually creating the store record. I flagged that this produces an impossible state — an approved application with no store behind it — and changed the flow so approval is store creation. I also replaced "delete" with "archive" for applications, so rejected history is never lost.

06 · The client storefront — where the taxonomy pays off

Client view

Thirteen sections — home, search with live states, catalogue, product page, cart, checkout, favorites, profile, purchases, reviews — all consuming the moderator-owned taxonomy. Every filter a shopper touches works only because no seller was ever allowed to type an attribute by hand.

07 · Every surface ships in light, dark and mobile

All three products were delivered with full dark-mode palettes and mobile layouts — not as an afterthought, but drawn screen by screen in the same Figma system, so engineering never had to invent a missing state.

Reconciling two truths

There was no usability lab — so every screen got validated against the client's own words instead.

Without users to test on, the real validation loop was showing the client two honest versions — what I thought was right, and what they'd actually written — and merging them in the open rather than picking a winner silently.

Loop 01

Categories page

My version used a hierarchical tree with breadcrumb drill-down. The client's literal spec wanted a flat A–Z list for fast scanning. Shipped both: a flat list for moderators who need to scan everything, with breadcrumb-aware drill-down into subcategories and views underneath.

Loop 02

Banners page

My version prioritized simplicity and reuse across banner types. The client's spec demanded specific fields per banner (name required, URL optional, uncompressed image upload). Final version kept their exact field list, with the simpler interaction model underneath it.

Loop 03

Sidebar grouping

Rather than asserting "this is correct," I presented 6-group, 7-group and 9-group versions side by side with the trade-off named explicitly, and let the client choose with full context — not just a recommendation to accept or reject.

"Every merged decision got tested against one rule: would the client recognize this in their own words?" — My working rule throughout the project

Strategic outcome

A shopper can filter consistently no matter which of hundreds of sellers listed a product. A seller fills predictable, constrained forms instead of guessing what a moderator will accept. A moderator works from navigation that matches how they actually triage — not how a database happened to be named.

The marketplace is in active development at umedmall.tj — currently onboarding partner stores ahead of public launch. Business metrics will be added once real traffic exists; I'd rather show none than invent them.

Lessons

What I'm taking with me into the next project.

What worked

  • Designing the attribute engine before the navigation. Once the data model was solid, the sidebar groupings followed almost on their own.
  • Showing trade-offs explicitly, not just a final answer. Presenting 6 vs. 7 vs. 9 sidebar groups made client sign-off faster because they felt ownership of the decision.
  • Treating "what's missing from the spec" as part of the deliverable. Units, input types, audit logs, cities and brands were never asked for — I added them because the product needed them, not because scope allowed it.

What I'd do differently

  • Get all three specs on day one. Receiving client, moderator and seller requirements in sequence meant revisiting IA decisions each time a new document arrived.
  • Lock the attribute/unit taxonomy before touching any screen. I designed individual pages first and had to retrofit the data model once edge cases like temperature surfaced.
  • Write the terminology glossary earlier. "Заявки" vs. "Заказы" vs. "Магазины" got renamed and re-grouped for weeks before the names settled — a glossary in week one would have saved that churn.
