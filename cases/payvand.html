Project Overview

One design system. Two apps built for two completely different people.

Payvand Group MDO is a micro-deposit organization in Tajikistan — licensed by the National Bank in October 2021, already running more than 3,000 payment terminals and 7,000 collection points, and moving from that cash network toward digital banking. The brief was «design a mobile app». The market said there were two separate problems, and one app would have solved neither.

The result: Payvand Wallet for registered users doing everyday domestic banking, and Payvand Transfers for migrant workers in Russia who just need to get money home tonight — no account required. One shared component library keeps them recognisably the same company.

The Problem

The competitor had already split its products. We had to answer that, not out-feature it.

The remittance gap

Labour migrants from Tajikistan working in Russia needed to send money home regularly. Most were not tech-savvy. The app they chose — Favri Intiqol, Kommertsbank Tajikistan’s remittance-only app, separate from its Favri mobile bank — won not by being feature-rich, but by asking almost nothing of them.

Kommertsbank Tajikistan did not try to serve them from its main banking app. It shipped Favri Intiqol as a separate product doing one job: money from a Russian card to a phone number or a Korti Milli card, zero commission, nothing else on screen. Everyone else — apps asking for an account, an ID check, a multi-step onboarding — watched those transfers go elsewhere.

«A competitor who ships a second, emptier app is telling you what the job actually is.»— My read of the competitive teardown, 2022

The domestic payments problem

Inside Tajikistan, NFC acceptance was barely deployed. Going through the local banking apps turned up the same complexity people already struggled with, and Alif mobi had set the habit everyone else was measured against.

Payvand needed to compete with that habit without just copying it. The insight: go where Alif hasn't — introduce QR payment as the country's primary cashless alternative to NFC, and build favorites/templates for the recurring bills people pay every month.

Research

The data said "one app." The users said "two different people."

I used Alif mobi as the primary design reference — not to copy it, but because users already had muscle memory for it. Understanding exactly what they relied on there, and what they found frustrating, shaped every navigation decision.

Method 01

Competitive teardown

Mapped Alif mobi feature by feature — what worked, where the friction sat, which habits users had already formed. Then laid Favri Intiqol over it to see why it was winning remittances: fewer screens, no account required, card in, card out.

Method 02

Market data analysis

Central Bank of Russia figures set the scale: $1.79B sent from Russia to Tajikistan in 2021, at an average of $128 per transfer. The World Bank put remittances at 27.8% of Tajikistan’s GDP that year. Numbers this size make the case on their own — a market this large, served by apps this hard to use.

Method 03

A Telegram room that grew by one owner at a time

Not user research — I want to be precise about that. When the project started we opened a Telegram group and added people to it only as questions came up: whoever owned the answer got pulled in. Over time the room became the project’s institutional memory, and polls inside it settled the questions no single person could. It is the closest thing to a spec this project ever had.

A note on data

The number that wanted to be the headline — and why it isn’t

Payvand’s team ran their own research and shared it with everyone working on the app. A market share for Favri Intiqol appeared in it: roughly 49% of Russia→Tajikistan transfers. It is not in this case study, and the reason matters more than the number.

Tajikistan publishes almost nothing. Companies do not disclose turnover — partly so the tax authority has less to ask about — and per-app industry statistics do not exist in any open form. So an internal figure here is unverifiable by construction: not because anyone lied, but because there is nothing to check it against.

Hence the rule this case is built on. Only numbers you can open in a link sit in the headlines — Central Bank of Russia, World Bank, the app stores. Internal estimates informed the work; they are not presented as fact.

And the two-app decision never rested on that 49% anyway. It rested on something anyone can still see: Kommertsbank had already moved remittances into an app of their own. An argument that needs a number you cannot show is a weak argument.

Key insights

Insight 01

Simplicity is a feature, not an absence of features

Favri Intiqol was not being chosen over richer apps. It was being chosen over apps people could not get through. Simplicity was the entire product.

Insight 02

Registration is a conversion killer for remittances

A migrant worker sending $128 home doesn’t want to create an account. They want to type a card number, verify the amount, and be done. Every extra step loses them.

Insight 03

QR is the viable cashless path in Tajikistan

NFC terminal penetration was near zero. QR codes, already deployed by merchants as static per-merchant codes, were the only realistic path to domestic cashless adoption.

The Strategy

Split into two products. Accept the cost of two apps to avoid the cost of one bad one.

The core decision

Putting both user groups in one app would mean compromising both experiences. A registered domestic user needs depth — balance, services, history, transfers, cards. A migrant remittance sender needs one thing: to get money to their family with as few barriers as possible.

One design system (Payvand UI) would ensure visual coherence and brand recognition across both apps, so they still felt like the same company — without forcing users who want simplicity to navigate complexity they'll never use.

Design goals

6 steps, no account

Payvand Transfer: direction → recipient and amount → card → confirm → SMS → done. Never ask for registration on the critical path.

QR as the cashless habit

Establish QR payment as Tajikistan's primary cashless method — build the habit before the infrastructure catches up.

One UI library, two apps

Payvand UI — cards, buttons, icons, type — applied consistently so both apps feel like Payvand, even though they serve different users.

Process

The four decisions that shaped both products.

Each of these was a direct response to something specific in the research — not a "best practice" applied generically, but a direct answer to a real user problem that the data had named.

1

Six steps, no account — and registration as a feature, not a gate

Payvand Transfer’s flow was designed to be completable without ever creating an account. Direction → recipient and amount → the card being charged → confirm → SMS code → delivered. Six steps, then done. Registration unlocks history and repeat-transfer features — a positive pull, not a mandatory barrier. This was the direct design response to why Favri was winning.

2

QR as the primary payment method — designing around the infrastructure gap

Rather than waiting for NFC terminals to proliferate, I designed QR payment as the first and most prominent service in Payvand Payments. In Tajikistan's existing merchant landscape, static QR codes per merchant were already the norm — customers enter the amount themselves. The design worked with that behavior rather than against it, and added favorites for the merchants people return to every week.

3

A shared component library, because there was nothing to inherit

Cards, typography, icon set, color tokens, button hierarchy — all built once and applied to both apps. A user who installs both apps recognizes the family instantly. The design system also solved the handoff problem: developers got one source of truth for both products, reducing inconsistency in implementation.

Design

Key screens and the reasoning behind each.

01 · Six steps — parity, not victory

Payvand Transfer

Corridor, recipient and amount, the card being charged, confirmation, the SMS code, done. Six steps, and registration is never asked for on the critical path. To be straight about it: this does not beat Favri Intiqol — it draws level with it. That was the point. On a flow this short there is nothing to win on cleverness; you either clear the same bar or you lose the person at step three. Being worse was the only outcome we could not afford.

02 · The rate is on the screen before you type the amount

Payvand Transfer

Under the amount field sits one line: «Комиссия до 99999 — 1,5%. Текущий курс 1 000 руб = 167,00 сомони». It is there before the first keystroke. A person sending money home is doing arithmetic — how much leaves, how much lands — and every app that hides the rate until the last screen makes them do that arithmetic twice. The catalogue of corridors does the same thing one level up: «Без комиссии» next to Payvand-to-Payvand, «Комиссия 1%» next to the paid ones. Price is a navigation label here, not fine print.

03 · Registration is a door further in, not a gate at the entrance

Payvand Transfer

The app opens on a guest home screen: four corridors, ready to use. The invitation to register sits underneath them — «Зарегистрируйтесь для полного доступа ко всем функциям» — where it reads as an offer rather than a toll. Someone who signs up gets a saved profile, history and a 4-digit access code; someone who does not still gets their money home tonight. This is the one place where we could have copied the competitor and instead went one step further: Favri Intiqol removed the account from the flow, and we made removing it visible on the first screen.

04 · QR is the first icon, not a menu item

Payvand Wallet

NFC acceptance in Tajikistan was barely deployed, but merchants already had static QR codes taped to the counter. So QR went first in the services row on the home screen — ahead of mobile top-up, utilities and everything else — because a habit that does not exist yet has to be visible before it can form. The model works with what was already there: the merchant has a fixed code, the customer enters the amount themselves, confirms, done. No terminal required on either side.

05 · A refusal is a screen too

Payvand Wallet

Two of the transfer outcomes are failures, and both were drawn rather than left to a toast message. «Недостаточно средств на счету» and «У этого контакта нет кошелька Payvand» get the same sheet, the same weight and the same single way out as the success state. The second one matters more than it looks: it is not an error the sender made, it is a fact about someone else, and saying so plainly is the difference between «the app is broken» and «try the card instead».

06 · Four runs at the same sign-in

Payvand Wallet

The file keeps four complete versions of the entry flow — start, sign-up, sign-in, verification — side by side. That is not indecision; a wallet lives or dies on the screen before anyone has seen anything of value, and it was worth redrawing four times. What survived: a phone number and nothing else on the first screen, the code auto-advancing across four cells, «Неверный номер?» as an escape hatch instead of a back button, and a 4-digit code you set yourself so the second session is one gesture.

07 · The components, briefly

Both apps

Both apps run on one shared library — card variants, button hierarchy, an icon set per payment category, colour tokens, and type sized for three languages of very different lengths. It lives in a separate file from the screens shown here, so I will not pretend to walk you through it. The point it earned: Transfer was drawn out of components that already existed, so the second app cost a fraction of the first and there was nothing to reconcile afterwards.

What shipped

Both apps are in the stores today.

The design covered both home screens, the core flows and the shared component library. Payvand Wallet now carries 250+ services, QR payment with cashback and three languages; Payvand Transfers handles Russia→Tajikistan and the rest of the CIS.

Strategic framing

The two-app decision was the design's central argument. Tajikistan's remittance economy was enormous and underserved by apps that prioritized features over simplicity. Both apps shipped — domestic banking via familiarity with Alif mobi, and remittances via a transfer experience built to the friction-free standard Favri Intiqol had set.

Lessons

What I'm taking into the next project.

What worked

  • Separating what I could prove from what I was told. The strongest version of the two-app argument turned out to be the one that needed no private numbers at all: the competitor had already shipped a second, emptier app. When a market publishes nothing, look for decisions competitors made in public — those are evidence you can still show a year later.
  • Designing the UI system before the second app. Building Payvand UI after Payments and before Transfer meant the second app was faster to design, with zero visual inconsistency between them.
  • Treating the competitor's strength as a design requirement, not a threat. Favri Intiqol won because of simplicity. The response wasn't to compete on features — it was to match that simplicity in the one context where it mattered most.

What I'd do differently

  • Test the 6-step flow earlier with actual migrant workers. The flow was designed from competitive analysis and interviews, but testing it with the specific audience — non-tech-savvy workers, not design-adjacent participants — would have caught usability gaps specific to that user group.
  • Define the QR merchant enrollment UX earlier. The consumer side of QR payment is clean. The merchant onboarding — how a small vendor in Dushanbe gets their static QR code — was left for later and would have shaped the consumer flow if designed in parallel.

Rather try it than read about it?

Both apps are in the stores. Install one, send something, and judge the flow with your own thumbs — that is a fairer test than any screenshot in a case study.
