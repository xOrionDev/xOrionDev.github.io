Project Overview

One design system. Two apps built for two completely different people.

Payvand MDO is a microfinance organization in Tajikistan building toward full digital banking. The brief was to design a mobile app. The research said there were actually two separate problems — and combining them in one app would have solved neither well.

The result: Payvand Payments for registered users doing everyday domestic banking, and Payvand Transfer for migrant workers in Russia who just need to send money home quickly — no account required. One Payvand UI library ties the visual language together.

The Problem

Favri won 49% of the remittance market with one feature: not asking questions.

The remittance gap

Labour migrants from Tajikistan working in Russia needed to send money home regularly. Most were not tech-savvy. The app they chose, Favri by Kommertsbank, dominated not because it was feature-rich — but because it demanded almost nothing from them.

Our data showed 49% of all Russia→Tajikistan transfers went through Favri, with over 40% of those through Komdmertsbank Tajikistan's system. Meanwhile the competition — apps that required account creation, ID verification, and multi-step onboarding — watched those transfers go elsewhere.

"Non-tech-savvy users chose Favri for one reason: simplicity. Simplicity was the product."— Research finding, competitive analysis 2022

The domestic payments problem

Inside Tajikistan, NFC payment infrastructure was essentially nonexistent. A survey of banking apps found the same complexity users already struggled with. Alif Bank had established the standard with Alif mobi — so strong that "Алифнуть" (to Alif) became a verb meaning "to transfer money."

Payvand needed to compete with that habit without just copying it. The insight: go where Alif hasn't — introduce QR payment as the country's primary cashless alternative to NFC, and build favorites/templates for the recurring bills people pay every month.

Research

The data said "one app." The users said "two different people."

I used Alif mobi as the primary design reference — not to copy it, but because users already had muscle memory for it. Understanding exactly what they relied on there, and what they found frustrating, shaped every navigation decision.

Method 01

Competitive teardown

Mapped Alif mobi feature-by-feature — what worked, what had friction, what "Алифнуть" actually meant in practice. Layered Favri on top to understand why it was winning the remittance market: fewer screens, no required account, card-in card-out.

Method 02

Market data analysis

Central Bank of Russia and TKB/PSB (TransCapitalBank, Promsvyazbank) transfer data confirmed the scale: $1.794B sent, $160 average, and Favri's 49% share. That share was only possible because migrant workers, not tech enthusiasts, were Favri's core users.

Method 03

User interviews

Interviews and surveys with users of both Alif mobi and competitor apps. Key finding: domestic users wanted familiarity and breadth; migrant senders wanted speed and as few barriers as possible. These were genuinely incompatible needs in one app.

Key insights

Insight 01

Simplicity is a feature, not an absence of features

Favri's users weren't choosing it over richer apps. They were choosing it over apps they couldn't navigate. Simplicity was the entire product.

Insight 02

Registration is a conversion killer for remittances

A migrant worker sending $160 home doesn't want to create an account. They want to type a card number, verify the amount, and be done. Every extra step loses them.

Insight 03

QR is the viable cashless path in Tajikistan

NFC terminal penetration was near zero. QR codes, already deployed by merchants as static per-merchant codes, were the only realistic path to domestic cashless adoption.

The Strategy

Split into two products. Accept the cost of two apps to avoid the cost of one bad one.

The core decision

Putting both user groups in one app would mean compromising both experiences. A registered domestic user needs depth — balance, services, history, transfers, cards. A migrant remittance sender needs one thing: to get money to their family with as few barriers as possible.

One design system (Payvand UI) would ensure visual coherence and brand recognition across both apps, so they still felt like the same company — without forcing users who want simplicity to navigate complexity they'll never use.

What each app needed to be

Payvand Transfer
  • Zero-registration path
  • Card-in, card-out (6 steps max)
  • Russia → Tajikistan, Uzbekistan
  • No feature noise
Payvand Payments
  • Full account features
  • QR, utilities, transfers, cards

Design goals

6 steps, no account

Payvand Transfer: recipient card → sender card → amount → CVV → confirm → SMS. Never ask for registration on the critical path.

QR as the cashless habit

Establish QR payment as Tajikistan's primary cashless method — build the habit before the infrastructure catches up.

One UI library, two apps

Payvand UI — cards, buttons, icons, type — applied consistently so both apps feel like Payvand, even though they serve different users.

Process

The four decisions that shaped both products.

Each of these was a direct response to something specific in the research — not a "best practice" applied generically, but a direct answer to a real user problem that the data had named.

1

Six steps, no account — and registration as a feature, not a gate

Payvand Transfer's transfer flow was designed to be completable without ever creating an account. Recipient card number → sender card → amount → CVV/CVV → confirm → SMS verification. Six steps, then done. Registration unlocks history and repeat-transfer features — a positive pull, not a mandatory barrier. This was the direct design response to why Favri was winning.

2

QR as the primary payment method — designing around the infrastructure gap

Rather than waiting for NFC terminals to proliferate, I designed QR payment as the first and most prominent service in Payvand Payments. In Tajikistan's existing merchant landscape, static QR codes per merchant were already the norm — customers enter the amount themselves. The design worked with that behavior rather than against it, and added favorites for the merchants people return to every week.

3

"Friends & Family" mode — solving social finance, not just utility finance

Payvand Payments included a social finance layer that competitive apps didn't have: debt tracking between contacts, group shared accounts, split-bill requests, and savings goals with automatic transfers. This was the long-term retention layer — features that make switching costly because your financial social graph lives in the app.

4

Payvand UI — a shared design system that made two products feel like one brand

Cards, typography, icon set, color tokens, button hierarchy — all built once and applied to both apps. A user who installs both apps recognizes the family instantly. The design system also solved the handoff problem: developers got one source of truth for both products, reducing inconsistency in implementation.

02 · QR payment — a new habit, not a new technology

Payvand Payments

The first icon in the services row. QR sits above mobile top-up and utilities, not buried in a menu — because building the habit requires surfacing it before the habit exists. Each merchant gets a static QR; the customer enters the amount. Favorites remember the merchants you scan every week.

The QR model in Tajikistan
  • Merchant has a static QR code pinned to the counter
  • Customer opens app → QR tab → scans
  • Customer enters the amount manually
  • Confirms, done — no terminal needed
Favorites & templates

Frequently-used payment accounts (mobile operators, internet, utilities) can be pinned as favorites on the home screen with optional auto-payment toggle — so the app learns the user's weekly payments instead of making them re-navigate every time.

04 · Payvand UI — the design system that made two apps feel like one

Design system

Rather than designing each app from scratch, I built a shared component library first: card variants (active, secret, add-new), button hierarchy, a custom icon set for each payment category, color tokens, and the distinctive blue gradient card design visible across both apps.

Strategic framing

The two-app decision was the design's central argument. Tajikistan's remittance economy was enormous and underserved by apps that prioritized features over simplicity. Splitting the product gave Payvand a credible path into both markets — domestic banking via familiarity with Alif mobi, and remittances via a transfer experience that matched the friction-free standard Favri had set.

Lessons

What I'm taking into the next project.

What worked

  • Letting market data lead the product strategy. The case for two separate apps came entirely from transfer data — without the remittance numbers, the argument for a separate simplified app would have felt arbitrary.
  • Designing the UI system before the second app. Building Payvand UI after Payments and before Transfer meant the second app was faster to design, with zero visual inconsistency between them.
  • Treating the competitor's strength as a design requirement, not a threat. Favri won because of simplicity. The response wasn't to compete on features — it was to match that simplicity in the one context where it mattered most.

What I'd do differently

  • Test the 6-step flow earlier with actual migrant workers. The flow was designed from competitive analysis and interviews, but testing it with the specific audience — non-tech-savvy workers, not design-adjacent participants — would have caught usability gaps specific to that user group.
  • Define the QR merchant enrollment UX earlier. The consumer side of QR payment is clean. The merchant onboarding — how a small vendor in Dushanbe gets their static QR code — was left for later and would have shaped the consumer flow if designed in parallel.
  • Design the empty state for Friends & Family from day one. The social finance features look compelling on a populated screen. An empty Payvand app has no friends, no groups, no debts — that's the most common first-session state, and it deserved as much design attention as the full state.
