Project Overview

The old app treated everything as equally important — so nothing was.

Dushanbe City Bank launched DC Wallet in 2018. By 2021 it was the bank's public face and its main channel to customers — and it was built like a folder of shortcuts. Eleven tiles of identical weight. No balance anywhere on the home screen. Paying for something sat next to "Favorites" as an equal.

The brief was to replace it with DC Next — an app that had to hold ordinary banking, city services, utility bills and public transport in one place, for a customer base that ranged from daily smartphone users to people opening a banking app for the first time in their lives.

I led the design end to end: information architecture, all core flows, the visual system and handoff. We were two designers; roughly 90% of the screens and the structural decisions were mine.

Before & after

Two questions the old home screen couldn't answer.

People open a banking app for one of two reasons: to see how much money they have, or to pay for something. DC Wallet's home screen answered neither without a tap.

DC Wallet · 2018–2021
  • No balance on the home screen at all — it lived two taps deep.
  • Eleven tiles of equal size and weight; "Pay" ranked the same as "Favorites".
  • The bottom of the screen held a title and two icons — no persistent navigation.
  • Services opened as a flat alphabetical list, the same for everyone.
DC Next · shipped 2022
  • Balance and bonuses sit at the top of the home screen, above everything else.
  • Four primary actions instead of eleven equal tiles; the rest moved into sections and a searchable catalogue.
  • A permanent five-tab bar with the QR scanner as its centre action, reachable from any screen.
  • Favourites fill themselves from what you actually use, and stay editable by hand.
What informed the decisions

No usability lab — but there was four years of real usage data.

I want to be precise about this: there were no moderated user interviews and no usability testing sessions on this project. What we did have was better than a guess and worse than a study — the bank's own usage data from the app it had been running since 2018, plus the operational knowledge of the teams handling support and payments.

Frequency isn't evenly distributed

A small set of services carried the overwhelming majority of transactions — mobile top-ups, utilities, transfers. Those earned a place on the home screen. Everything else went into a catalogue with search, instead of competing for the same visual space.

…but every customer has their own small set

Ranking by aggregate frequency fixes the average user and fails the specific one. Someone paying the same kindergarten bill monthly gets no benefit from a global top-nine. That contradiction is what Favourites exists to resolve.

A first-time user and a daily user share one screen

The customer base spanned people paying with QR every morning and people installing a banking app for the first time. The home screen had to be immediately legible to the second group without slowing down the first.

Key decisions

Four decisions that shaped the rest of the app.

01 · Balance above everything

The card balance, the currency and the bonus total moved to the top of the home screen as a single block. It sounds obvious written down; it was the largest structural change in the redesign, because it demoted the service grid from "the home screen" to "one part of the home screen" — and that demotion is what made it possible to cut eleven equal tiles down to four primary actions.

02 · One scanner, three different jobs

Paying a merchant, topping up a wallet by QR and paying a bus fare are three different mental tasks — but technically they are the same act: scan a unique code, confirm an amount. Rather than build three entry points there is one scanner — one tap from the home screen, later promoted to the centre of the tab bar — and the flow after the scan adapts to whatever the code turns out to be.

Honest attribution: the unified QR standard itself was the bank's payment infrastructure, not my design. What I owned was the decision to expose it as a single always-present action instead of three separate features, and the interface of the flow that follows the scan.

03 · Favourites that fill themselves

A section that starts empty stays empty — most people never curate anything. So Favourites populates automatically from what the customer actually repeats, and remains fully editable: remove what you don't want, pin what the algorithm missed. The global ranking serves the average customer; this serves the specific one. Two mechanisms, one screen, no contradiction.

04 · Statements, version two

The first statement screen shipped as a correct list of operations that nobody could read quickly. The second version separated the statement from the operation log, added grouping and filters, and made a single transaction expandable into full detail with a repeat action. Two versions of the same screen exist in the file — I'd rather show both than pretend the first one was right.

Designed, not shipped

"Lend to a friend" — the feature that didn't make it.

A peer-to-peer lending flow: send money to someone you know with an agreed return date, tracked by the app instead of by memory. It was designed in full and never shipped — priorities moved elsewhere.

I'm including it because a portfolio of nothing but wins is a portfolio that hides how the work actually goes. The flow is sound; the decision not to build it was the bank's, and it was a reasonable one at the time.

What came after me

The app kept growing after 2022 under the team that took over: a mini-apps layer with third-party services, face-unlock security, sign-up through Telegram, parking and EV-charging partners. None of that is my work. The navigation model it all sits on — balance first, permanent tab bar, scanner in the centre — is.

Lessons

What I'd keep, and what I'd do differently.

What worked

  • Ranking the home screen by measured usage, not opinion. It ended the recurring argument about whose department deserved a tile.
  • Treating the scanner as navigation, not a feature. Putting it in the tab bar meant every later payment type inherited an entry point for free.
  • Automatic Favourites. Personalisation that requires no setup is the only kind most people ever get.

What I'd do differently

  • Insist on talking to users. Usage data tells you what people did, never why they stopped. On a product this size, even ten interviews would have changed decisions.
  • Design the statement screen last, not first. I built it early from the data model and had to rebuild it once real transaction histories made it unreadable.
  • Record the reasoning as I went. Four years later the screens survive but the arguments behind them don't. I now write decisions down while they're still being made.
