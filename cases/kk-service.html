AI + Human Credit Desk

Designing the internal tool Alif Bank's credit committee uses to process loan applications — an AI scoring engine clears most cases automatically, and a human can step in at any moment to catch what the model missed, or get help making the harder call.

Project Overview

Ten channels fed one credit committee. None of them spoke to each other.

Loan applications reached Alif Bank's credit committee from ten different systems — the online banking app, the partner shop platform, the CRM, the core banking system, and more. An AI scoring engine could resolve most applications automatically. The ones it couldn't — or shouldn't, like a possible-fraud flag — needed a human, fast, with full context.

My job was to design one queue that kept the person waiting in a branch visible to the reviewer, split the human work across the right specialists, and made sure risk signals could never be silently auto-approved.

I designed the full system end to end: journey mapping across four employee roles, the unified application list, the client profile that a decision is made from, and the admin panel that configures it all.

The Problem

Automation that hides its own work is automation nobody trusts.

Business reality

Applications arrived from ten different source systems, each with its own ad hoc tracking. There was no single queue, no shared vocabulary for status, and no consistent way to tell which cases the scoring engine had already resolved versus which ones a human still needed to touch.

Three things had to be true at once:

  • Most applications had to clear automatically, or the committee would drown in volume.
  • A human had to be able to intervene on any case, at any point — to fix a model mistake, or get help on a hard one.
  • Certain signals — possible fraud, litigation, a deceased applicant — could never be silently auto-approved, no matter what the model said.

Ten channels, one queue (where they landed)

Before this project, an application's origin determined how it got tracked. Unifying these into one list was the precondition for everything else — automated triage, queues split by how the client arrived, and a visible handoff between engine and human.

Grounding the system

I mapped four jobs before designing a single screen — three reviewers and the person who configures their queues.

A loan decision isn't one task. It's a relay: someone verifies the documents, someone verifies the identity, and someone reviews the credit case itself — each requiring different judgment. Getting that division of labor right, before touching pixels, shaped everything downstream.

Method 01

Journey mapping, four roles

Built a step-by-step journey map for each of the document checker, the identity verifier, the credit reviewer, and the admin — twelve concrete steps per role, from "receives the case" to "hands off to the next stage."

Method 02

Channel audit

Traced applications back through all ten source systems to see exactly where tracking fragmented, and what data each channel actually carried by the time it reached the committee.

Method 03

Risk-case stress-testing

Walked through every risk flag — possible fraud, litigation, a deceased applicant, a flagged extremist match — to confirm none of them could resolve through the automatic path, only through a human decision.

Key insights

Insight 01

Automation needs to be legible

If a reviewer can't tell at a glance which cases the model already resolved, "human override" becomes guesswork instead of collaboration.

Insight 02

One application, three different jobs

Document verification, identity verification, and credit review require different expertise. Forcing one generalist queue to do all three slows everyone down.

Insight 03

Risk flags are interrupts, not noise

A possible-fraud or litigation flag has to stop automation cold, every time — it can't be one more badge lost in a busy queue.

"The model isn't the reviewer's competitor. It's the first reviewer. The UI's job is to make that handoff visible." — The framing I kept coming back to
Define

The brief was "let AI handle most applications." The real problem was making the other ones easy to catch.

Problem statement

The committee needed a single queue where most applications resolve through scoring, every human override is fast and fully informed, and certain risk signals always interrupt automation — regardless of how confident the model is.

That meant designing for three audiences at once: the model's output, the specialist reviewing a specific stage, and the admin configuring how work gets distributed.

Goals

  • Make automation visible — every case tagged as auto-resolved or needing a human, before anyone opens it.
  • Split the queue by how the client arrived — walk-in, online, returning — because a person sitting in the branch and a form submitted at night are not the same job.
  • Make risk flags non-negotiable — fraud, litigation, deceased, extremist-match always force a human checkpoint.
  • Unify all ten channels into one list with filters reviewers already understand: client status, application status, amount, city, source, date.

What "done" had to mean

Origin visible at a glance

Every card shows auto vs. manual origin without opening it — no clicking through to find out who (or what) decided.

No silent risk approvals

Fraud, litigation, deceased and extremist-match flags always force human review — never resolved by the automatic path.

Context survives every handoff

A mandatory comment travels with the case at every stage transition — document check → identity → credit review.

Process

The decisions that made AI and humans actually collaborate, not just coexist.

None of these were about making the model better. They were about making its work legible enough that a person could trust it, question it, or take over from it in seconds.

1

Assembling one person out of five systems that had never spoken

This was the hard part, and none of it is visible on a screen. A decision needs the applicant as a whole: the branch queue system knows they are sitting in the office right now; the CRM holds who they are; the National Bank's credit bureau holds what they owe other banks; the Alif super-app holds what they earn and spend; and somewhere in all that sit dependents, a car, whether the flat is owned or rented. Five sources, five formats, none built to answer a credit question. The design problem was deciding what a reviewer sees of that pile — and in what order — when the model has already read all of it. The reviewer needs the same picture the engine used, arranged for a human in a hurry, not a data dump proving the system did its homework.

2

The timer that keeps a waiting person on screen

A row in the list does not just show an application — it shows how long its owner has been sitting in the branch. «Ожидание клиента: 23 мин.» The number comes out of the branch queue system, which had no reason to talk to a credit tool until this project. It changes what a reviewer optimises for: a form submitted online at night and a person in a chair three metres away look identical in a database and are not remotely the same job. The timer is what makes that difference impossible to miss.

3

Queues split by how the client arrived, not by what the task is

Walk-in, online, returning client — three queues, because those three situations have different clocks. The walk-in queue is measured in minutes against a person who is physically waiting; the online queue in hours; the returning client already has a history that answers half the questions. Splitting by arrival, rather than by verification step, is what let the branch case be processed in roughly half the time it used to take.

4

Risk flags that can't be auto-resolved, by design

Possible fraud, active litigation, a deceased applicant, an extremist-list match — these are modeled as interrupts, not statuses. No matter what the scoring engine returns, a case carrying one of these flags routes to a human, every time.

5

Removed a Telegram bot from the review process — for security, not aesthetics

Committee review used to run through a Telegram bot: links and comments sat in every front-office specialist's chat history, and stayed there after they left the bank. I replaced it with an electronic queue inside the CRM itself — oldest-first ordering, a visible color signal for how long a case has waited, and a fixed set of question templates instead of free-form chat messages. The security fix and the operational fix turned out to be the same redesign.

03 · Voting — where the score becomes one input among the committee's own votes

Auto + human

The decision isn't one button — it's a vote. The scoring engine's read sits in the same list as each committee member's vote, never hidden behind the final outcome. A reviewer can agree with the score, vote against it, or request more information, and every vote requires a comment that travels back to the client's record and the CRM.

04 · The queue — what replaced the Telegram bot

Security redesign

Offline applications sort oldest-first. The longer a case waits, the redder its marker gets — a reviewer doesn't need a report to see what's been sitting too long. Opening a case from the queue dims its row and shows who's looking at it.

Validation

For a system this consequential, validation meant compliance sign-off as much as usability.

Lending decisions carry real regulatory and fairness weight. The review loops here checked not just "can people use this," but "can a risk signal ever slip through," and "does the queue split match how staff actually specialize."

Loop 01

Compliance review

Walked every risk flag with compliance stakeholders to confirm none of them — fraud, litigation, deceased, extremist-match — could resolve through the automatic path under any model output.

Loop 02

Ops walkthrough, three roles

Validated the document/identity/credit split against how staff actually specialized day to day — not how I'd assumed the work divided on paper.

Loop 03

Admin configurability check

Confirmed duty rotation and queue structure could flex as headcount and volume changed, without requiring a new release for every staffing adjustment.

The campaign that nobody had to be rescued from

Alif runs four lending campaigns a year — two broad, two targeted, like the auto-loan push that drops the rate to 11%. Since 2020 every single one has driven application volume past forecast, and every single one ended the same way: staff pulled in from other departments to help the credit team clear the backlog. The first campaign after KK Service went live, that didn't happen.

These figures come from the bank internally — shared with me by the head of the credit department — and are not published anywhere I can link to. I'd rather say that plainly than present them as independently verifiable.

Strategic outcome

The credit committee can absorb far more volume because the AI genuinely carries most of the routine load — while every reviewer always knows whether they're double-checking a model or making the only real call on the case in front of them. Risk signals can't get lost in that volume, by construction, not by vigilance alone.

Lessons

What I'm taking with me into the next project.

What worked

  • Mapping all four roles before any screen. The document/identity/credit split came directly from the journey maps, not from an assumption about how the work "should" divide.
  • Making automation visible instead of hiding it. The single "Auto" tag did more for trust between reviewers and the model than any amount of accuracy messaging could have.
  • Treating risk flags as interrupts, not badges. Designing them to structurally block automation, rather than just display a warning, meant compliance sign-off was straightforward.
  • Fixing a security problem and an operations problem with the same redesign. Replacing the Telegram bot wasn't pitched as a UX improvement — it was a data-leakage fix that happened to also remove the spam, lost applications, and untracked wait times the bot caused.

What I'd do differently

  • Define the auto/manual tagging convention before any individual screen. I had to retrofit the badge across cards and detail views once the pattern solidified.
  • Bring compliance in earlier. The risk-flag review happened after the core flows were drafted, which meant revisiting decision logic that could have been right the first time.
  • Stress-test admin configurability with real headcount numbers from day one. I designed the duty-rotation screens generically, then had to adjust once actual team size and shift patterns came in.
  • Treat the Service↔CRM sync as core scope, not an integration detail. QA surfaced a long tail of desync bugs — edited amounts, calculator adjustments, and committee comments not reliably round-tripping back to the system of record. That sync layer needed its own design attention from week one, not a retrofit.
